Progress report · September 2026

India Rainfall 3D U-Net

Correcting ECMWF's 32-day rainfall forecasts over India with a 3D U-Net, and checking honestly how much it helps.

What we are doing

ECMWF publishes a rainfall forecast for the next 32 days. Beyond the first week it is not very accurate. We train a neural network to take ECMWF's forecast and output a corrected forecast, and check it against rain actually observed by the India Meteorological Department (IMD).

The network is a 3D U-Net ported from a published model for the western US (Ryu et al. 2026; their code). The main findings so far:

  • The model beats raw ECMWF and bias-corrected ECMWF at every forecast day.
  • The standard score overstates skill. A forecast that just predicts the long-term average beats raw ECMWF on it, so we also score with the average removed.
  • Predicting the departure from normal, with a small "trust gate", gives the best result so far: +0.025 correlation over the baseline.
  • The model's rainfall maps are too flat: they carry about half the real variability.
  • 12 of the 17 inputs add nothing measurable.

Inputs and outputs

Each training example is one ECMWF forecast, identified by the date it was issued. The model sees ECMWF's forecast for the next 32 days plus one real observation, and outputs corrected daily rainfall for the same 32 days.

The input has shape 17 × 32 × 48 × 64 and the output 1 × 32 × 48 × 64:

The model

"3D" here means forecast day × latitude × longitude. One network sees all 32 days at once, so what it sees at days 1–20 can inform its correction at day 21. The network shrinks the data three times to find large-scale patterns, then expands it back, with skip connections carrying fine detail across.

  • Each block: two rounds of 3×3×3 convolution, normalisation and activation.
  • Loss: squared error + (1 − pattern correlation), over Indian land cells only.
  • Training: Adam optimiser, stops when the validation score has not improved for 10 epochs.
  • Scaling: inputs are rescaled to average 0, spread 1, using training years only. The paper's code computes this scaling over training, validation and test years together; we changed that when porting.

The model has 22.4 million parameters and 724 training examples, so it overfits early. The best epoch is usually 4–7.

How we score it

ScoreWhat it measuresPerfect
Pattern correlationDoes the predicted rainfall map match the observed map? Our main score.1
RMSEAverage error in mm/day0
EpreThe paper's combined score of correlation and variability0
SD ratioVariability of the prediction ÷ variability of reality. Below 1 means the map is too flat.1

Climatology removed. Much of an Indian rainfall map is predictable from the calendar alone (the west coast is wet every July). So we also score after subtracting the long-term average for that date and place from both the prediction and the truth. What remains is the part a forecast has to actually predict.

The long-term average always uses earlier years only: a 2010 forecast uses 1951–2009, never later years.

Experiment 3

Departure from normal, and the trust gate

Instead of drawing the whole rainfall map from scratch, the model predicts how far this forecast departs from normal. A "gate" lets it decide, for each place and each forecast day, how much to trust ECMWF.

normal     = average rain for this date and place, earlier years only
ecmwf_dev  = ECMWF forecast − what ECMWF usually forecasts for this date and day

the network outputs two maps: gate (0 to 1) and correction

predicted  = normal + gate × ecmwf_dev + correction

We ran four versions. The three new ones use 672 training forecasts: 2004 has no earlier years of ECMWF forecasts to build its "normal" from, so it was dropped. The Baseline is the 724-forecast model from Experiment 1. Gate A, the plain head and the Baseline all get ECMWF rain as an ordinary input. In Gate B it can only reach the output through the gate.

The four versions, written out

Every quantity is per grid cell and per forecast day. Rain is handled as log(1 + rain), so the "departures" below are departures in log-rain. All four use the same U-Net body; only the inputs, the target and the last layer differ.

obs        = log(1 + IMD observed rain)                      what we score against
ecmwf      = log(1 + ECMWF rain, average of 11 members)
normal     = average of obs for this day of year,  years before this one only
ecmwf_usual= average of ecmwf for this issue date and forecast day,  earlier years only

obs_dev    = (obs − normal) / σ_obs                           how unusual the real rain was
ecmwf_dev  = (ecmwf − ecmwf_usual) / σ_ecmwf                  how unusual ECMWF says it will be

In versions 2–4 the normal is subtracted from the target before training and added back after the network runs, so the network only has to learn the departure. The loss (MSE + 1 − pattern correlation, Indian land cells only, all 32 forecast days) is computed on what the network outputs: rescaled log-rain for version 1, obs_dev for versions 2–4. The other 15 input channels (spread, humidity, winds, pressure, heights, SST, yesterday's rain, calendar) are the same in all four, except that in 2–4 yesterday's rain is also given as its departure from normal.

The gain splits roughly in half between predicting the departure from normal and adding the gate. On RMSE all four versions are within 0.1 mm/day, which is within retraining noise. The gate improves where the wet and dry areas are placed, not the average size of the error.

The gate does not behave as designed

In Gate A the gate stays near 0.15 on day 1 when it should be about 0.53. The network gets ECMWF rain through its ordinary inputs as well, so it relies on that route. When the ordinary route is removed (Gate B), the gate moves toward the ideal, but the overall score falls back to the Baseline level. The gate helps as a direct shortcut from ECMWF's forecast to the output, not as a readable "trust" value.

Every run here was trained once, and the gaps are about twice the difference between two runs of the same setup. Repeating them with different random seeds is the first next step.

Review response

Professor's comments

Everything here uses the 16-input model without yesterday's observed IMD rain. Observed IMD rain would not exist at forecast time, so it is no longer an input; IMD is used only as the training target and for scoring. Experiment 6 showed that removing it leaves the standard score unchanged (0.443 vs 0.443). Images are collapsed: click a heading to open one, or click an image to open it full size.

Which inputs feed one predicted value?

The whole input block: 21–109.5°E, 19.5°S–39°N, all 32 forecast days and all 16 channels. The network is convolutional, so there is no hand-picked window. Three pooling steps let one coarse cell summarise 12° × 12° × 8 days, and skip connections add the fine local detail back. We measured the influence with gradients (day-14 output at Nagpur and Mumbai, averaged over the 153 test forecasts):

Summary

  1. The model beats raw and debiased ECMWF at every forecast day. Its real skill, with climatology removed, is 0.33 averaged over 32 days: 0.55 at day 1, 0.15 at day 28.
  2. The standard score overstates skill, because climatology alone beats raw ECMWF on it.
  3. Predicting the departure from normal with a gate is the best version so far (0.357 vs 0.332).
  4. Predictions are about half as variable as reality.
  5. Adding the 12 atmospheric inputs (humidity, winds, pressure, heights, SST) to the base five does not improve the score. Removing yesterday's observed rain changes little: no effect on the standard score, a small unconfirmed gain with climatology removed.
  6. Yesterday's observed rain is no longer an input, because it would not be available in real time. The review plots show the model overfits after about epoch 7, runs 1.2–1.7 mm/day too dry, and is worse than raw ECMWF at the all-India weekly amount even though it is better on the spatial pattern.
